I've always had so many interests or passions, that I found it impossible to choose one, indefinitely. I often found myself envious of those who knew what their "one true calling" in life was, and focused on that one thing exclusively.

The belief is:

> You can either be a jack-of-all-trades, or you can be a master of one.

And I always felt that I was at the wrong end of that continuum.

But what if we don't need to choose one mode or the other?  
What if you could do many diverse things in your life, without scattering your force and starting over every time you switch from one field to another?

## Scanners & Renaissance Souls

Sunni, a Gingko user in California, has been a follower of this blog for some time, and often emails me with commentary. A couple months ago, he saw in me something that we both shared: a love of diversity, of pursuing many interests. Of being what Margaret Lobestein calls a "Renaissance Soul", and what Barbara Sher calls a "Scanner" in her book *Refuse to Choose*.

Even in the titles and summaries of the books Sunni recommended, I immediately recognized myself:

> What Sher has discovered is that some individuals simply cannot, and should not, decide on a single path; they are genetically wired to pursue many areas. Sher calls them "Scanners"--people whose unique type of mind does not zero in on a single interest but rather scans the horizon, eager to explore everything they see. - Summary of *Refuse to Choose*

.

> Are you unwilling to settle on doing just one thing “for the rest of your life”? Do you jump at the chance to learn something new—or, after achieving success in one field, find yourself yearning for new challenges and looking around for something different to do? If so, you may be a Renaissance Soul. ... Author Margaret Lobenstine offers a life-planning strategy perfectly in tune with those who fear becoming “a jack-of-all-trades and master of none.” - Summary of *Renaissance Soul*

Both books do two things: first, they legitimize this alternate mode of being. I haven't felt this much recognition since reading the incredible "Quiet: The Power of Introverts...", where I saw how the world has touting the Extrovert Ideal, and marginalizing the introverts of the world.

With "Renaissance Soul" and "Refuse to Choose", I realized the same thing is true: the world has glorified those that choose one path/career/calling, and vilified the "amateurs" who choose to do many things.

The message of all these books is simple, but powerful: it's OK to be who you are.

The second thing these books do, is offer strategies and systems to use, that allow you to manage all your disparate interests, without feeling overwhelmed or boxed in.

And yet, something feels amiss...

I've always believed, and seen, the power of focus. One thing at a time. Single-handling the most important task until it's done.

My desire to "focus on one thing at a time" was so strong, and so *internally driven*, that I couldn't ignore it. It doesn't feel like I'm being "pressured to choose", it feels like I yearn to focus on one thing.

## The ONE thing

Before I read the "Renaissance/Scanner" books, my desire for focus lead me to finding, and devouring, books on single-mindedness. None was more powerful, or more *straight-forward* than "The ONE Thing: The Surprisingly Simple Truth Behind Extraordinary Results".

The ideas are incredibly simple:  
1. Fact: You can only do one thing at a time.  
2. Fact: Some things matter a great deal more than others (the Pareto 80/20 rule).  
3. Fact: Some things are leverage, and make everything that follows easier or unnecessary.  
4. Therefore: you can achieve the greatest results by repeatedly asking, and answering, the following question:

> "What is the ONE thing I can do, such that by doing it, everything else will be easier or unnecessary?"

The author uses the analogy of knocking over dominoes. Every domino you knock down can actually cause a domino that's 50% larger to fall. No big deal? Following this progression, the twelfth domino is X, the nth is Y, and the zth is Z. Your goal is to find that first domino, and whack at it until it falls.

One of the oldest productivity gurus, Brian Tracy, recommends much the same thing. Steve Pavlina of "Personal Develpment for Smart People" credits a similar approach for being able to finish four years of university in a year and a half.

More importantly, when I started implementing these principles, they *felt right*. The case for finding and doing my ONE thing is very compelling.

And yet, it seems to be at odds with a fundamental aspect of myself: my desire to pursue and do a great many things.

In a sense, these are polar opposite modes: "Refuse to Choose" give you permission to go as broad as you like and to do a little of everything, without worrying or caring about finishing *anything*. "The ONE thing" requires you to focus on only one single thing, to the near-exclusion of all else, until it's done.

Both feel right to me.

And perhaps, both *are* the right approach, for *everyone*. But only when used in the right combination.

## Exploration and Commitment

I've talked about "souls" and "callings" long enough. It's time for my favorite device: the mathematical analogy!

In physics, when you have a system (say, a bunch of atoms in a box), the number of ways that they can be arranged grows incredibly quickly. This is called the "Curse of Dimensionality".

How do you find the "best" arrangement in space of possibilities that's so vast, there are often more ways of arranging the system than there are atoms in the universe?

1.  You pick any starting point.
2.  You compare it to a random point nearby.
3.  If it's better, move there.
4.  If it's not better, roll a dice.
5.  If the roll is higher than a particular risk threshold, move anyway. If not, stay put.
6.  Repeat

It sounds preposterous, but it works. Why?  
Because, *Science*!

Anyway, the important point for our purposes is the risk threshold. Too low, and the system ends up stuck anywhere that's "not too terrible", but will never explore far enough to find a better spot. Too high, and the system will just bounce around all states, never staying in any one place long enough, even if it's pretty good.

Starting to see the link?

One more analogy, this time a little closer to our experience. Imagine that you were at a casino, facing 20 different slot machines, sometimes called "one-armed bandits". You have a fixed amount of money, and you want to get as much reward as possible. How do you do so?

Well, in real life, the best way is to **walk right out of the casino**. But assuming you had to gamble: how do you choose which machines are best to play, when *you don't know* which have the best odds?

Well, you start by playing *all of them*, until you get a sense of which ones yield better rewards. *Then you focus* on those until your money runs out.

This is called the "Multi-armed bandit" problem in computer science. There is no exact solution for all cases, but all solutions involve strategies that balance the **exploration** of all machines, with the **exploitation** of the ones that seem best so far.

## A Different Default

The difference between people who are Jacks-of-all-trades, and those that are Masters-of-one, is simply a different default mode. A different preference between exploration and commitment.

> But if your goal is to find the pursuits that will give you the most rewards in wealth, joy, and contribution, *you will fail* if you use either mode exclusively.

We revere those who reach the pinnacle of any field, and one cannot do so without focus. So we assume that focus is what leads to results.

But how many people "focus" on the wrong career, and spend their entire lives doing something they hate? This is a **failure of exploration**. It's a fear of stepping outside the comfort of the one thing that kind of works, and forgoing the chance to find something else that fulfills you. The comfort zone extends up to the bounds of their initial choice, and they do not stray from it.

On the other hand, we can spend our lives jumping from one interest to the other. We can "Refuse to Choose", and draw excitement from many various areas.

In doing so, we may continuously experience the joy of learning, but we are unlikely to stick long enough to any one path to be able to make any significant contribution to the world. This is a **failure of commitment**. The comfort zone extends broadly to all fields, but never deep enough into any one of them to experience challenge and growth.

In both cases, I believe the fear stems from the same source: a fixed mindset.

If you have a fixed mindset, and your default is *to commit*, you eventually reach the "OK plateau" of your field, and stay there. You don't push yourself to climb higher, nor do you push yourself outwards to new things. You're stuck, counting the years and days until retirement when you can "be free".

If you have a fixed mindset, and your default is *to explore*, then your set of problems are different. You pick something up, learn the basics quickly, but stop at the first major challenge. You don't push yourself to move past the obstacle, to where the rewards are. Instead you find a new passion, and repeat the cycle. This leads to a meandering life, full of unrealized potential.

## A Dynamic Balance

So how do you balance exploration, and commitment?  
Let's turn again to the mathematical analogies.

In the Monte Carlo world, one powerful way to make it more likely that you'll find the overall best state, is to start the system with a very high temperature (i.e. high risk tolerance), and allow it to jump around rapidly, exploring the whole space. And then, you **gradually reduce the temperature** over time, leading you to spend more time in promising areas.

For the multi-armed bandit, the approach is the same. Begin with complete focus on exploring all the slot machines. But gradually start to give preference to the ones that yield more returns. Towards the end, you'll be focusing exclusively on the best machines.

If your goal is single-minded and crystal clear, and you are following the path of the ONE thing, you still need to pause between every domino and use divergent thinking to widen your options and decide your next step.

If your goal is to accomplish many diverse things, you still need to commit to one at a time, see them through, and reap the rewards, before moving on to the next.

> Whether you want to do one thing, or many things, you will always need to balance exploration with commitment.

## Why isn't this common sense?

If we were completely free, this is what we would do naturally. As children, we would follow our curiosity, explore the areas we're drawn to, and gradually focus on fewer as time went on. Perhaps narrowing it down to one for life, or one for every few years.

The reason it's difficult to actually do this in our society, is simple. Amidst abundance, we still feel scarcity. We need to do things for money.
