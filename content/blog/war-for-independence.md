There are many things I love to do. I love spending time with my family. I love writing and coding, designing, thinking, and learning. There are many things I hate to do. I hate selling, optimizing for profit, accounting, networking. I hate bureaucracy, competition, following trends, or following the news.

Fortunately, I am in a position where I can do far more of what I love, than what I don't.  

> And that's a position I want for everyone.  
> I believe it's possible.

  
However getting to that point is a constant battle. It's a battle because many of the systems we are embedded in require us to trade independence for security. What is a job, after all, than a trade? *"I give you some of my freedom, you give me some of your money."*

It's harsh, but true. The degrees of freedom you give up vary, and if you love your job, you might gain more than you lose.

But in the end, if you want to do *what you love, and only what you love*, you're going to have to fight for it. No one is going to give you your independence.

It is a war, but it's an internal war.  
And it's one that you can win... but you have to decide to take part.  

## The Example of the USS Benfold

  
I first realized the power of waging war on what you dread doing, from the books “*Good to Great*” and “*Decisive*”, both of which recount the following story.

Captain D. Michael Abrashoff took command of the guided missile destroyer USS Benfold, of the US Pacific Fleet. One of his first actions was to interview every one of his 310 crew members, asking them:  

-   
- What do you like most?
    
- What do you like least?
    
- What would you change if you could?
    

  
From these interviews, the Captain created two lists. List A contained everything that was critical, important, fulfilling and challenging work. List B contained everything that was important, but not essential. “the dreary, repetitive stuff, such as chipping and painting.”  

> “After compiling the two lists, Captain Abrashoff  
> **declared war on List B**.” - Decisive, chapter 9.3

  
One of the most dreaded tasks was painting the ship, so one sailor suggested replacing the navy’s standard issue ferrous-metal bolts on the hull with stainless-steel ones. Because the navy didn't supply them, they actually went down to Home Depot and Ace Hardware stores across San Diego and bought every bolt they could find.

A second dreaded task was the scraping and sanding of certain upper parts of the ship, which tended to corrode. The crew discovered that there was a way of protecting the metal from corrosion, but the navy couldn't handle the volume of metal the crew needed to process. Instead, they tracked down a steel-finishing firm that could do the job for \$25,000.

It seems like a lot of time and money was wasted on this. After all, ship crews had been sanding and painting their hulls as regular parts of their duty for decades. But it paid off.

“The sailors never touched a paintbrush again,” said Captain Abrashoff. “With **more time to learn their jobs**, they began boosting readiness indicators all over the ship.”

With more time to do the challenging and important work that the crew loved,  

> the Benfold crew was able to complete the navy’s standard six month training exercise **in the first week**, and earned **a higher score** than any other ship...

  
including those ships that completed the entire six months.

Now, though the external result was great (record-breaking results), I argue that the internal result (crew fulfillment & learning) was more important.  

> *When you focus only on what you love,  
> great results are a nice side-effect.*
