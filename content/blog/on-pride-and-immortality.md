Most people I observe around me are driven by the desires for money or pleasure. The default philosophy of life is one of “enlightened hedonism”. I'm driven by none of those things. I genuinely do not care about money beyond basic needs.

I know that many artists, scientists, academics, and open-source software enthusiasts often say the same thing. In these realms, we focus on the false dichotomy of “money” vs “freedom”. On “selling out” or “staying pure”.

So if not money, then what drives us?  
For me, and for many people like me, the answer is this:  

> I want to change the world. I want to leave my mark, make a dent, make a difference.  
> **I want to be remembered.**

  
These statements may have a nobler air.  
But I've realized recently, it’s just *a different kind of lust*.

<!--more-->

Instead of wealth, it's a lust for immortal fame. The basest form of fame, the kind we can't even enjoy in the present.

I'm ashamed to admit that I've long believed that I could make an impact great enough to be remembered. And it's **one of the most damaging beliefs** I have. It is holding me back from both joy and, ironically, from producing truly good work.  

## Where does the belief come from?

  

> "You have so much potential!  
> You can be anything you want to be."  
> - Parents, everywhere

  
It starts early, subtly, and is borne of the best intentions.  
Parental praise sets the seed.

As a parent, I understand. Watching a child grow at first fills you with a wonder for life in general. But soon it becomes about your child in particular. *My* son is special. He is wonderful and unique. Slowly, without even realizing it, my wife and I start saying things like "Isn't he just the *best*?"

While the<img src="http://imgs.xkcd.com/comics/physicists.png" class="alignright" width="358" height="540" /> seed is often planted by parents, they are not accountable for it. Long after leaving the home, we carry and nurture the belief on our own as well, for years or decades afterwards.

In the sciences in particular, the pull of immortality is very strong. As a physicist, you are exposed to a long tradition of naming fundamental elements of the universe after the people who discovered them. Fermions (after Enrico Fermi), Schrodinger's equation, Brownian motion, the Lagrangian, the Hamiltonian.

At the same time, physicists acquire the annoying habit of looking at everything in the world as fundamentally reducible (see Fig \[1\]). The combination can lead to a tremendous amount of hubris.

## Why is it damaging?

There are two problems with believing you can achieve immortality through your work. First, to achieve this kind of immortality, *you must by definition* choose work based on what others people value, not on what you yourself value.

I'm ashamed to admit that many times I've been pulled into a seductive problem, not because it was inherently interesting to me, but because solving it would "guarantee" lasting fame. I've dabbled with Fermat's theorem, tried my hand at the Riemann hypothesis for prime numbers, and many other such problems.

Not for the sake of solving the problem, but *to be known* as the one who solved it. And I had the misfortune to have enough imagination and perseverance to make some progress, but inevitably hit a road block. And when I did, I didn't have the strength to break through it, because I was doing it for all the wrong reasons.

Most importantly, believing that you can achieve lasting recognition through your work places you firmly in an "expectation gap". You imagine what you could accomplish, look at what you have accomplished, and feel the weight of the span between them.  

## What does it *mean* to change the world?

  

> “We are here to make a dent in the universe.  
> Otherwise, why even be here?”  
> - Steve Jobs

  
According to Steve, our goal in life is to make a profound difference in the universe. And if we don’t, why bother existing at all?

I’m sorry Steve, but **that’s a terrible philosophy of life**.

Let me share something with you:  
![](http://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/69/NASA-HS201427a-HubbleUltraDeepField2014-20140603.jpg)

This isn’t the whole universe. This is just **one 24-millionth of the whole sky**. Every one of those blobs and dots aren’t stars, but entire *galaxies*.  

> Steve Jobs did not dent the Universe, and neither will I.  
> And neither will you.  
> **And it’s OK**.
