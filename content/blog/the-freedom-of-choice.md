My reaction to his obsession exposed a **flawed belief** I had. One that affected all my choices, including how I wrote, how I worked, and how I chose to live.

And the same flawed belief might be hiding under *your* very own consciousness!  
♪♫*dun-dun-duuuuun*♪♫

If you've ever had fear of committing to a project, career, or relationship, then read on and see if you believe what I did.

Since he was a baby, Z has watched BBC nature documentaries. Now he's riveted when watching the ins and outs of snake & reptile breeders on YouTube. Everything from how to tell if a mutation is co-dominant, to how to replace an alligator pond filter pump. We had been to the museum's reptile exhibit so often, that the staff recognized him and gave him a special backstage tour, letting him hold rare (harmless) snakes.

There's much to love about this. He's learned a great deal about snakes in particular, and it provides an easy hook for other lessons (genetics, physiology, evolution, environmental sciences, etc). Since I'm homeschooling him, that's a great benefit!

Instead of encouraging it, though, I realized I had subtly been trying to steer him *away* from snakes over the last few months.  

> I was doing the opposite of what I should have been doing as a teacher: I was trying to dampen his curiosity. Why?

  

### The Big Crunch: Continual Contraction

  
My argument to myself was this: he's too young to be *this* narrowly focused. And why is that a problem? Because there are whole vistas of knowledge, culture, and experience, that he hasn't the faintest idea of yet. What if there's something "better" for him, that he'd enjoy more, but he never gets to find out it even exists?

Suddenly, I realized that in this argument there was an underlying belief, that goes something like this:

*"At the beginning of life is the time to play: to explore interests widely and freely. The next stage is to slowly narrow your attention as you grow older, towards what gives you the most joy. In the end, you find the One Thing you want to dedicate the rest of your life to doing, and get really focused on that. In doing this, you maximize your chances of long-term satisfaction."*

It sounded very sensible to me \[1\]. **But it's also very claustrophobic**. It feels like the space of possibilities is constantly getting smaller as you move through life.

<img src="/blog/images/Screenshot_2017-12-13_22-40-43.png" class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-1323" width="780" height="273" />

There are a number of corollary beliefs that came along with that one.  

1.    
2.  There is a "global optimum", and it's possible to find it if one looks long enough, with the right strategy.
      
3.  Choices in life are finite. The more choices we make, the fewer choices we "have left".
      
4.  If you are an adult, and you haven't found your One Thing, well, you're in trouble.
      

  
Combined, this network of beliefs make up an important part of the perfectionist mindset that I've struggled to get over for so long. Here's how it can show up in writing, as a concrete example:  

1.    
2.  There is one ideal expression of my work, and it's possible to achieve if I explore and mull over the options long enough.
      
3.  I have a finite number of "works" in me, so I better not "waste" one on something that isn't the "right" one.
      
4.  I am most free now, at the beginning of the project, when there is "so much potential".
      

  
The results of this mindset are: fear, doubt over actions, and the inability to commit to any one thing. It's part of why picked up a 1001 hobbies while growing up, but hardly ever finished a project in any one of them.

Fortunately, this belief is neither right nor wrong. It isn't really in the realm of the provable... which is liberating, because it means **we're free to choose an alternative**, if a better belief becomes "available" to us.  

### Alternative 1: Continual Expansion

  
Often the hardest parts about modifying a belief, is uncovering it in the first place. Once I realized that I had this "continual contraction" belief, reversing it was simple. Here's my first attempt:

*"At the beginning is the time to choose One Thing to focus on. The next stage is to slowly build on what you've learned, then expand your view, and choose something else. Eventually, you'll be able to explore your interests widely and freely."*

<img src="/blog/images/Screenshot_2017-12-13_22-40-52.png" class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-1324" width="779" height="285" />

This has a number of benefits over the "continual contraction" model. Starting with one clear thing and then expanding, is a benefit because it frees us to focus on other aspects of ourselves that pay massive dividends over the course of a lifetime.

Here are two ways that this "continual expansion" belief improves over its opposite.

**1. Learning to Struggle**

Having a clear target can teach you **how to push past resistance**. It's no coincidence that so much of what we've learned about peak human performance comes from the world of competitive athletes. These are people who've put aside all thoughts of "*what* should I be doing?", and put that energy towards "how can I do *better*?".

The important thing to realize here, is that **the goal itself almost doesn't matter**. Once you learn to aim for something, and push past your comfort zone in order to grow and achieve it, that lesson that transfers to anything else you want to achieve. In a sense, you're *learning to learn*.

**2. The Possibility of Return on Investment**

Seen in a purely dry and "economic" sense, every thing you do can either give you back less than, more than, or equal to the time and energy you put in \[2\].

Finishing something might end up being a time investment... you put in 2 years into a novel, but that yields enough of a residual income that gives you just a little more freedom for the rest of your life. Or, it might not, in which case you've "only" received the benefits of learning to struggle, and of learning to learn.

However, *if you don't finish* a discrete piece of work, you are **guaranteed to have lost** that time and energy. This is true whether you stop at 10% completion, or 90% \[3\].

My point isn't that we should focus on "maximizing our ROI". My point is that the benefits of learning to struggle, learning to learn, and the possibility of having more time/energy later are only possible if we aim for completion.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

There is, however, one major flaw to the "continual expansion" belief. Taken to it's extreme, it's actually very familiar pattern in our culture: work hard at something (anything) for a few decades, and then reap the rewards and freedom during retirement.

I am completely opposed to that pattern, as I've seen it lead to massively regretful lives. Committing to it turns the only life you'll ever have into a single random draw... if you were lucky, then the work was fun, and the retirement long and worth it. If you weren't, well then you're out of time and there are no do-overs.

Both alternatives, taken to their extremes, are paralyzing in their own way. So I tried to find a third way.  

### Alternative 2: Exploration-Commitment Cycles

  
In this model, we have two modes: **Exploration** and **Commitment**. We can cycle through them throughout life, at a pace that suits our temperament. We can also have different cycles for different aspects of our life at the same time: for instance, we can be exploratory when it comes to our work, and at the same time committed when it comes to our primary relationship.

<img src="/blog/images/Screenshot_2017-12-13_22-43-35.png" class="aligncenter size-full wp-image-1325" width="782" height="284" />

The exploration phase is where we follow whatever we find interesting, flit from one thing to another. Sometimes settling on something for a time, but always holding things lightly. We aren't "supposed" to be doing anything in particular. What we learn here is the shape of the world, and more importantly, we learn what appeals uniquely to us.

The commitment phase is where we choose to set an intention, and stick to it. It's where we build character and integrity. It's in this phase that we learn to struggle, and that we grow. At times, it is frustrating and is nothing but work. But at other times, it can also be free flowing and feel like play.

Eventually, we learn just how, where, how often, and for how long, to stay in each of these modes. But being conscious of which mode you're in is critical. When you're committed, you can lay to rest all thoughts of exploration until you're "done". When you're exploring, you can do so without fear, until you're ready to commit. And in each, there's the understanding that the mode won't last forever, and that both are needed for a full life.

I believe that this is a far more natural way to live, and allows for the greatest total freedom. Here's how this belief is structured.  

1.    
2.  There are literally infinite paths through life, and the only way to move through life is to make those choices \[4\].
      
3.  Choices never run out, and making choices now can lead to more choices later.
      
4.  It's never too late, or too early, to be in either mode.
      

  
To make things more concrete, here's how this might play out in your work as a writer.

*Let's say we find you in an exploratory phase. You are "between projects", and can follow your interests wherever they lead. Nothing is too far out there, and you are free to start and stop anything at will. You are drifting, and as long as it's temporary, it's a good place to be. But eventually you're pulled towards something. There's an idea you want to solidify, or a story you want to get out. And slowly you start to focus in on that.*

*So you decide that now, it's time to commit. You set a specific goal, and visualize clearly what you want. It won't be easy, but you know that you'll grow regardless of the outcome, precisely because it isn't easy. This stage has a cadence all of its own, and a different type of freedom: the freedom to explore <span style="text-decoration: underline;">within</span> the bounds of the project. You work consistently, knowing that every word you actually put down, even the ones you end up discarding, are moving you towards your goal: completion. There is struggle, and frustration, and it would be here that you'd normally give up... but you can't, because you made a clear commitment, and the commitment pulls you through.*

*Finally, inevitably, you finish. It's done. It looks very different than what you expected, but you're happy with it. It's not perfect, but it's right.*

*It's now time to breathe in again, and explore.*  

### Conclusion

  
These are the changes I've made in my life, to try to reinforce this new belief.  

1.    
2.  I've reinstated my "7th week off" rule. Every 7th week, I drop all expectations of my work, and allow myself to explore freely. I did this for almost a year at one point, but somehow I forgot how important it was.
      
3.  I am allowed to start projects as often as I like, but with the understanding that they're not "really" started if I'm still exploring.
      
4.  Conversely, I'm much firmer about finishing those that I have committed to.
      
5.  And finally, I'm going to allow my son to get as deep into snakes as he wants to, for as long as he wants to.
      

  
Most importantly, if I really learn to replace my belief in "The Big Crunch" with a belief in "Exploration-Commitment Cycles", I'll finally be able to let go of many of the fears that hold me back from building my life, one choice at a time. Because, what it comes down to is this.  

> Freedom doesn't come from <span style="text-decoration: underline;">having</span> choices.  
> Freedom comes from <span style="text-decoration: underline;">making</span> choices.
